# Chapter: The Word of a Saint and the Word of the Lord

## A Fundamental Question

What is the word of a saint, and what is the word of the Lord? How do we tell the two apart? I want a clear definition of this.

Many sayings come before us. One may be a universal principle, and another may have been written by a saint; it may be a commentary or note on a scripture, or a sentence from the life story of a saint. There is a straight formula to identify which is which: is this the original universal principle, or did some guru add it later? Neither will be wrong, but there is certainly a difference between the two.

## The First Rule: The Words of the Lord Are Universal

Understand this carefully. The words of the Lord will be universal. That is the first rule.

Universal means that the word will apply in India, in Pakistan, in America, and on the moon too. It will apply anywhere across the countless universes. This is called **Paravani**, the word of the Supreme Reality.

It has to be applicable everywhere. Whatever the religion, whether a person is dark or fair, tall or broad, a king or a beggar, a saint or a thief, it applies to all alike. Is this first part clear?

## The Word of a Saint: According to Place and Time

The word of a saint can sometimes be the universal word too. A saint may say things that are exactly the word of the Supreme Reality, applying everywhere.

But the work a saint comes to do is for a particular region and a particular time, and the words that come only for some social reform there should also be understood as an application of the universal principle. Yet they may be limited to time and place, for a short period or for a particular area. When such words come, that part is temporary.

## An Example: Celibacy and Rules of Dress

Take an example. If celibacy (brahmacharya) is to be taught, is it a universal principle? Will it apply everywhere? Yes. So when saints teach about celibacy, they too are teaching something universal.

But if in the Middle East, in the Arab countries, some guru says that women should wear the burqa, then tell me first: is wearing the burqa compulsory everywhere? Their saints must have given that rule after some thought, for their people, for their climate, according to their people's behaviour. Here too, in our times, it must have been said that women should keep the end of the sari over the head, keep a veil (ghoonghat), and so on.

Now tell me, is the point that had to be made the same in both places or not? Modesty, celibacy, something like this is what both had to say. But there the rule became the hijab and the burqa. This is what is called the word of a saint.

Did their guru teach something wrong? Did the guru here teach something wrong? No. One only has to understand what is called the universal word, the word of the Lord, and what is called the word of a saint.

> "The word of a saint can change according to time and place, but the deep essence it is meant to convey will be universal."

You have to hold on to that universal point. But we first grab the outer differences, the methods and the rituals, and holding on to them, people fight and kill one another.

## The Three Parts of Every Religion

Take any religion, and it will have three parts.

1. **The essence:** The very first part is its fundamental principles, the foundation on which the religion stands. Its essence will be one and the same for all: the Supreme Reality, the Self. The gurus of every religion, that is, its realised masters, those who were avatars, know this.

2. **Story or Purana:** Those great beings lived in their own era and their own region, so each has a story. A life story forms around them. This is called mythology, or among us, the Puranas.

3. **Rites and rituals:** Then, so that the ordinary person can bring it into life, rites and rituals are created and given.

This final essence is given to people in the form of stories, rituals, rites, worship and yajna, so that you too may return to that very essence, which is common to all paths. The essence of all three religions is one. Their stories will differ, and their rituals will differ.

## Why We Get Stuck in the Lower Two Parts

The ordinary person still does not understand this non-duality (advaita). So he sees only the lower two parts. He says that Jesus Christ's clothing was different, his region was different, his words were different, his language was different. Among us, Krishna's clothing is different, his words are different, his region is different. So everything is different.

Then we see the rituals. Someone will bow in one way, someone in another, and we in a third.

> "We fools get stuck right there."

And this is not only a problem of Hindus. The ordinary people of every religion, who are still caught in duality (dvaita), stop at these lower two parts only.

## If You Do It, Do It Properly

Now do you see where the problem is? So if you do it, do it properly; otherwise do not do anything.

In India the problem is that we teach the child a great deal of religion, but we do not give its meaning at all.

What is the problem abroad? Their religion itself is limited. What does limited mean? It is limited to one book, the Bible or the Quran; it is limited to one God, Allah or Christ. So there may be fewer quarrels among them. Still, the person who wants to quarrel will quarrel anyway; there will be Protestants and all the rest, something or other will keep happening. We are not discussing that. But the chances are fewer. Here, from the very start, we have kept fifty different ones, and thirty-three crore deities as well.

## Making Our Own God Small

When you quarrel that my God is greater than your God, at that very moment you make your own God small. So say the scriptures.

When you say that your chosen deity (ishta devata) is smaller than mine, have you not made your own chosen deity limited? Yours is limited, and his is limited too. You have created duality. Knowingly or unknowingly, he makes his own chosen deity, who was infinite, limited. So say the scriptures.

That is why this saying was spoken at the start:

> **sādhakānāṁ hitārthāya brahmaṇo rūpakalpanā**
> "For the good of the seekers, a form is conceived for Brahman."
> (Rama Purva Tapaniya Upanishad 1.7, common reading)

Brahman is infinite and formless; form is for the convenience of the seeker. So to fight over forms is to limit that very Infinite.
