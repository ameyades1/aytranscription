# The True Essence of Dharma: How the Word of Saints Differs from the Word of God

We have all learned a great deal in the name of **dharma** (religion, the right way of living): rules, stories, ways of worship. But have you ever stopped to ask which of these teachings holds for all time, and which belonged to a particular time and place?

This discourse opens with a direct question. How does the word of the saints differ from the word of God, and how can we tell the two apart?

## Who Spoke These Words?

Many kinds of teachings reach us. Some state a **universal principle**. Others are a saint's commentary or remarks, or a line from the story of a saint's life.

Gurudev says there is a simple rule for telling them apart. Neither kind is wrong, but they are not the same.

## The First Rule: God's Word Holds Everywhere

The words of God are **universal**. That is the first rule.

Universal means they hold true in India, in Pakistan, in America, and even on the moon. They apply anywhere in the countless universes of creation.

This is called **Paravani** (the word of the Supreme). Whatever your religion, whether you are dark or fair, a king or a beggar, a saint or a thief, it applies to you exactly as it applies to everyone else.

## The Saint's Word: Shaped by Time and Place

At times a saint speaks that very same word of the Supreme, the truth that holds everywhere.

But saints also speak to the region, the era and the social reform they came for. These teachings still apply a universal principle, yet they may be bound to a particular **time and place**. That part of their message is temporary.

## One Example Makes It Clear

Teaching **brahmacharya** (celibacy and self-restraint) rests on a universal principle that holds everywhere. When saints teach it, they are teaching something universal.

Now suppose a teacher in the Arab lands told women to wear the burqa. Is the burqa required everywhere? No. Those saints would have given that rule after looking at their people, their climate and the customs of their society.

In our own land, too, people were once told to keep the **pallu** (the end of the sari drawn over the head) and to wear the **ghunghat** (a veil over the face). In both places the point was the same: **lok-lajja** (modesty and a sense of propriety before society) and brahmacharya. Over there, the rule simply took the form of the hijab and the burqa. This is the word of a saint.

Did their teacher teach something wrong? Did ours? No.

Acharya Ji explains that a saint's word can change with time and place, but the deeper truth it carries remains universal.

*Of the religious practices you hold on to most tightly, how many are the essence, and how many are only the rule of a particular age?*

## The Three Parts of Every Religion

Look at any religion and you will find three parts:

1. **Fundamentals**: the foundation on which the religion stands. It is the same for all: the Supreme Reality, the **Atman** (the Self). The self-realised Gurus and incarnations of every religion know it.
2. **Mythology**: the stories and lives of its great figures, set in their own age and their own land.
3. **Rituals**: the worship, prayers, fire offerings and ceremonies that help ordinary people bring the teaching into daily life.

All of these were given so that we could find our way back to the one essence that every path shares. The essence is one; only the stories and rituals differ.

## Where Do We Get Stuck?

Ordinary people cannot yet grasp **Advaita** (non-duality), so they see only the two outer layers.

They say: Jesus wore different clothes, lived in a different land and spoke a different language. Krishna's clothes, words and homeland were different too, so everything must be different. Then they look at the rituals: one person bows this way, another that way. And we, foolishly, get stuck right there.

This is not a problem for Hindus alone. In every religion, those still caught in **dvaita** (duality) stop at these two outer layers. Then people cling to these outward differences and end up killing one another over them.

## If You Do It, Do It Properly

Gurudev puts it plainly: if you are going to do something, do it properly, or do not do it at all.

The problem in India is that we teach children a great deal of religion but never explain what it means.

Abroad, religion is usually limited to one scripture, such as the Bible or the Quran, and one God, whether Allah or Jesus. That leaves somewhat less room for conflict, though those who want to fight will fight anyway. Here, from the very beginning, there have been fifty different forms of the Divine and thirty-three **koti** (traditionally counted as crore, or ten million) deities.

## When You Make Your Own God Smaller

The moment you say, "My God is greater than yours," you make your own God smaller. So the scriptures say.

By comparing, you have confined your **ishta devata** (your chosen deity). You have created duality. Knowingly or not, you have put limits on the Infinite.

> **sādhakānāṁ hitārthāya**
> **brahmaṇo rūpakalpanā ||**
> "Forms of **Brahman** (the Absolute) are conceived only for the good of spiritual seekers."
> (Ramapurvatapaniya Upanishad 1.7, as commonly cited)

A form is there to help the seeker. Brahman itself has no end.

*Does your devotion lead your chosen deity toward the Infinite, or does it trap it in comparison?*

## Key Takeaways

- God's word (Paravani) is universal: it applies in every land, in every age and to every person.
- A saint's word is sometimes universal, and sometimes serves the social reform of a particular time and place.
- Rules like the burqa and the ghunghat differ, but the principle behind them (lok-lajja and brahmacharya) is the same.
- Every religion has three parts: fundamentals, mythology and rituals. The essence is the same for all.
- Those caught in duality see only differences in stories and rituals, and fight over them.
- If you teach religion, teach its meaning too: if you do it, do it properly.
- Saying "my God is greater" confines your own infinite chosen deity.
