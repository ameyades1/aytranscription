# From Praise to Self-Knowledge: How Sanatan Dharma Evolved

Have you ever wondered why we worship at all? Where did the custom of **dakshina** (the offering given to a priest or teacher) come from? And how did we end up with so many deities, each with its own ritual for a particular wish?

In this discourse on the evolution of Sanatan Dharma, Acharya Upendra Ji takes us on a journey through the Vedas: from the Mantra Samhita to the Brahmanas, the Aranyakas and finally the Upanishads. The story is more than history. It invites you to understand what **dharma** (the spiritual path, the right way of living) was really meant to achieve.

In short, Sanatan Dharma grew through the four parts of the Vedas. In the Mantra Samhita the rishis praised God and nature; in the Brahmanas that praise became action, through yajna, charity and worship; the Aranyakas brought reflection in the forest; and the Upanishads gave the final knowledge, the knowledge of the Self.

## What Is the Mantra Samhita? When the Rishis Thanked Nature

The first part of the Veda is the **Mantra Samhita**, a collection of hymns offering **stuti** (praise) to God.

Acharya Ji paints a lovely picture. A guest arrives at your home. You say, "Please come in, have a seat," and then spend two hours doing nothing but praising them: the title they received, the dance competition they took part in, how well their business is doing. Then you say, "That is enough praise for today. You may leave now." Would that really work?

In those days, the four **varnas** (the social classes of ancient India) were assigned according to a person's aptitude. Farming depended on the rain, and those with a Brahmin's temperament had time on their hands, a calm mind and few desires. The king was busy governing, the Vaishya with trade, and the Shudra with labour.

In their solitude, the rishis watched nature closely: how faithfully the sun rises every morning, how the river flows without pause. A feeling welled up within them: "The time has come for me to show my appreciation." That praise turned into composition on its own, and it became the Mantra Samhita.

## Are Words Enough? The Birth of the Brahmanas

Then a question arose. When a guest comes to your home, what good does it do merely to say, "You are wonderful"? At the very least, offer them a sweet, give them water, and help them wash their hands and feet.

That is how the second part of the Veda came about: the **Brahmana**, the part devoted to **kriti** (action). The sixteen-step and five-step forms of worship, **shodashopachara** and **panchopachara**, grew out of this same impulse. Yajna (fire offering), charity, austerity, ritual service and worship together make up this action-oriented portion of the Veda, which is called the Brahmana.

Take the example of the sun. At first people prayed, "O Aditya, O Sun God." Then that felt like too little, so they began offering water. Pouring it from above, they drenched whoever stood below, and complaints followed! Eventually the sun came home in the form of an idol, and since nobody could walk to the river every day, one new ritual after another took shape.

The Brahmin varna takes its name from this part of the Veda as well. A Brahmin is someone who walks the path of **Brahman** (the ultimate reality), whose single aim is to move towards it.

## Love Shows in What You Do

Acharya Ji ties this principle to everyday life: love expresses itself in action.

Suppose a wife says, "I love you very much, but I will not make you tea." That does not work. And if someone sits all day with a rosary chanting "honey, honey, honey," their wife will leave for someone else!

Shri Krishna gave Radha gifts that befitted his royal stature, such as Kusum Vihar, Radha Kund and Krishna Kund. A king who says "I love my people" while taxing them heavily does not love them. When he builds roads and provides for their comfort, that is true love. Love should be unconditional, and it should shine through in what you do. Otherwise it was never love at all.

*Does your love for the people closest to you live only in your words, or does it show in your actions too?*

## The Roots of Dharma: Nature and the Ancestors

Swami Vivekananda describes this history beautifully in his works on Bhakti Yoga and Karma Yoga. Religion began with the **worship of nature** and the **worship of ancestors**.

At some point, the first human being must have died. People wondered, "He was right here yesterday. Where has he gone?" That question led to a search, the search led to action, and action led to **shraddha** (rites performed for departed ancestors) and **pinda** (rice-ball offerings made to them). Even today, at a shraddha we speak well of the departed (praise) and perform the rites (action).

The deities came from the same source, because people worshipped what they could see. Sadhana (spiritual practice) follows the same path: first **sakara** (God with form), then **nirakara** (the formless). The Brahmins thanked nature in their huts and ashrams. Was there anything wrong with their intention? Not at all.

## "You Must Offer Your Own Thanks"

When kings and Vaishyas saw the rishis gaining **siddhis** (spiritual powers) and learning, they grew curious. The rishis told them everything. But the king, ruled by **rajas** (restless, ambitious energy), said, "I have no time. You do it for us. Do it to protect me." The Vaishya said, "Do it so that I prosper. I will give you dakshina."

The rishis replied that they were giving thanks for what nature had given them, and the king would have to offer his own thanks.

They told him, "I can teach you this knowledge, just as it passed from the Sun to Manu and from Manu to Ikshvaku. I can be your guide." But the king said, "We have far too much work. You do it. I will give you this many cows."

In the end, the assembly of Brahmins decided, "Free cows are on offer, so take them." According to the scriptures, this is how dakshina began. Over time, a whole science developed for performing worship on someone else's behalf. Maharshi Patanjali explained that when you meditate on another person's mind, an exchange of **tapasya** (austerity) takes place: you give them some of your spiritual merit and take on their poison. For a while everyone was happy, yet nobody actually learned anything.

## Why Did Worship Become Mechanical?

Three or four generations later, the Brahmin forgot whom he was worshipping for, and the king forgot why he was paying dakshina. On both sides, everything had become **mechanical**.

The other classes then noticed, "All he does is offer flowers. I can do that myself." They picked up the outward ritual but missed its purpose, which was **atma-tattva** (the essence of the Self). The Brahmins began to lose their livelihood, and they said, "We have to evolve further."

*Has your own worship turned into a mere habit, or do you still remember why you do it?*

## Many Gods, Many Desires

Worship now began to divide along the lines of desire: one ritual for a house, another for a vehicle, yet another for a son. New deities, new mantras and new procedures appeared, each with its own specialists.

Acharya Ji makes it clear that none of this was meant to trap people. It was genuine **evolution**, much like scientific discovery. "Necessity is the mother of invention": survival was at stake, so the intellect developed.

Then another four or five generations passed, both sides forgot once again, and it all turned into the billion-dollar business we see today. People came to believe, mistakenly, that nothing works unless you pay a certain amount. And once again, the growth of the Veda came to a halt.

## Yajnavalkya: The Sage Who Gave Back All He Had Learned

That is when **Rishi Yajnavalkya** appeared. Though born into a Brahmin family, he raised a question: "What was the purpose? **Self-realisation**. Now it has become nothing but a business."

His Guru replied, "Even if it seems wrong, you must do it. This is the tradition." Yajnavalkya said, "I cannot accept that." The Guru said, "Then return the knowledge I gave you."

The story goes that he **vomited** it out, symbolically giving back everything he had learned. Then he went into meditation and discovered higher truths: truth does not lie in ritual alone, and something exists beyond it.

The established faction opposed him, and with the help of a king driven by rajas, they made it the rule. According to Acharya Ji, this led to a split. The Brahmins' path came to be called the **Shukla Yajurveda** (the "white" Yajurveda), while Yajnavalkya's path was branded "dark and wrong" and called the **Krishna Yajurveda** (the "black" Yajurveda). That is why, even today, the Shukla Yajurveda contains more ritual, while the Krishna Yajurveda pairs its procedures with more reflection.

He was cast out and had to go to the forest. Yet he kept searching, and when the time came, he returned that knowledge to society.

## From Aranyaka to Upanishad

The knowledge born in the forest became the **Aranyaka** portion of the Veda. From it arose the **Brihadaranyaka Upanishad**. The **Chandogya Upanishad** blends the two and speaks of ritual, the body and the Self.

The sequence, then, looks like this:

1. **Mantra Samhita**: praise
2. **Brahmana**: action and ritual
3. **Aranyaka**: reflection in the forest
4. **Upanishad**: knowledge of the Self

## Shri Krishna's Call: Rise Above the Gunas

> **traiguṇya-viṣayā vedā nistraiguṇyo bhavārjuna |**
> **nirdvandvo nitya-sattva-stho niryoga-kṣema ātmavān ||**
> "The Vedas deal with the three gunas. Rise above them, O Arjuna. Be free of the pairs of opposites, ever established in sattva, free from concern for gain and security, and centred in the Self."
> (Bhagavad Gita 2.45)

Here the verse becomes a call to go beyond the three **gunas** (the qualities of nature: sattva, rajas and tamas) and learn the essence of the Veda directly.

- **Nirdvandva**: rise above the pairs of opposites, such as cold and heat, gain and loss, victory and defeat, honour and insult.
- **Nitya-sattva-stha**: give up the activities of rajas and **tamas** (inertia, dullness), and dwell always in **sattva** (purity). You will find that purity in the presence of a true Guru.
- **Niryoga-kshema**: let go even of the worry over **yoga** (what you have yet to gain) and **kshema** (what you need to protect). Your Guru will take care of that.

> **ananyāś cintayanto māṁ ye janāḥ paryupāsate |**
> **teṣāṁ nityābhiyuktānāṁ yoga-kṣemaṁ vahāmy aham ||**
> "Those who meditate on Me with undivided devotion, I Myself carry what they need and protect what they have."
> (Bhagavad Gita 9.22)

## The Scripture That Says, "Do Not Read Me"

Acharya Ji points out something startling. In the second chapter of the Gita, just before these verses, Shri Krishna states plainly that anyone who stays entangled in the ritual portion of the Veda is a fool. The Veda is the only scripture in the world that calls out, "Do not read me."

Yet Vedanta is itself part of the Veda. As Acharya Ji puts it, out of a hundred pages you were meant to skip ninety-six and go straight to the last four.

In other words, you were meant to find a true Guru and learn from them that final knowledge, the knowledge of the Self.

*Which pages of your spiritual life are you still stuck on, and are you ready to move on to the last four?*

## Key Takeaways

- The Vedas evolved in four stages: the Mantra Samhita (praise), the Brahmanas (action), the Aranyakas (reflection) and the Upanishads (knowledge of the Self).
- Love shows itself in action, not just in words; this is the heart of the Brahmana portion.
- Dharma began with the worship of nature and of ancestors, because people first worship what they can see: from form to the formless.
- Dakshina arose because people driven by rajas did not want to practise sadhana themselves; the rishis' message was, "You must offer your own thanks."
- Over the generations, both worship and dakshina became mechanical, and their purpose, the essence of the Self, was lost.
- Rishi Yajnavalkya stood against ritual that had turned into a business, kept searching in the forest, and gave the world the wisdom of the Aranyakas and Upanishads.
- Shri Krishna urges you to rise above the three gunas and the pairs of opposites, stay rooted in sattva, and leave the worry of gain and security to the Guru.
- The real goal is to learn the final part of the Veda, Vedanta, from a true Guru.
