# Chapter: From Praise to Self-Realisation — The Evolution of Sanatan Dharma


## Introduction: The Veda as a Living Journey

We often think of the Veda as a closed book, written once and for all. In this chapter Acharya Upendra Ji presents the Veda as an **evolving journey** — in which human beings first praised nature, then added ritual, then saw that ritual turn into a business, and finally, through one sage's rebellion, arrived at the Self-knowledge of the Upanishads.

The four parts of the Veda — **Mantra Samhita, Brahmana, Aranyaka and Upanishad** — emerged one after another in this order. Acharya Ji explains each part with a simple, homely example, and at the end shows, through the Bhagavad Gita, which part of the Veda the seeker has to reach.


## 1. Mantra Samhita: Praising the Guest

The first part of the Veda is the **Mantra Samhita**:

> "The first part is called Mantra Samhita. Mantra Samhita means offering praise to the Supreme Lord, and so on."

To explain it, Acharya Ji asks us to imagine a guest:

> "A man is coming — your guest, an *atithi*, isn't he? We invited him with great respect, we called him in. So think of it at home: he arrives, and it's 'Come in' — and for two hours you only speak nice things."

> "And now: 'That's enough praise, now go' — would that work?"

How was this praise born? In the society of that time, when there was no year-round business but farming, people of Brahmin intellect had time and a calm mind:

> "They would think: how does this nature run, how sincerely it works — that is all they observed — how the river keeps flowing. **It is high time I should appreciate.**"

The other varnas were busy with their own work, but the rishis and munis went into solitude:

> "So the rishis and munis meditated on nature — how sincerely it comes and goes, how the sun comes. **While praising it, composition happened on its own**, and it was converted into the Mantra Samhita."

In other words, the first part of the Veda did not come from a command; it sprang spontaneously out of **gratitude** towards nature.


## 2. The Brahmana Portion: Love Is Expressed in Action

But praise alone does not complete hospitality to a guest:

> "Arre, if someone has come, then at least something — offer a laddu, give some water, give something. If they have come, give them something, then we'll talk."

From here arose the second part of the Veda, the **Brahmana** — from which the methods of worship came:

> "From that arose **shodashopachara puja, panchopachara puja** and so on — all of that, some kriya (ritual action)."

(*Shodashopachara* — worship with sixteen offerings; *panchopachara* — worship with five offerings.)

Acharya Ji describes with light humour how this ritual kept growing — offering water to the sun, then an idol at home:

> "Physically he cannot eat anything. Arre, the water doesn't reach up there — it falls on the head of the person on the floor below, and there's a complaint! Then in one's own house — then he came into the idol."

> "So **some new kriya kept on evolving**. That part is called Brahmana."

The name of the Brahmin varna is connected to this too:

> "One who walks the path of Brahman is called a Brahmin; he has only one aim — to go towards Brahman. So the action part — **yajna, dana, tapa, offerings, worship — that part of the Veda is called Brahmana.**"

Acharya Ji gives the essence of this portion in one sentence:

> "If a guest comes, praise alone won't do; along with it some action is needed too. **Love is expressed in action.**"

And he drives it home with examples from life:

> "The wife said, 'I love you very much, but I won't make tea for you.' Saying that doesn't work."

> "Shri Krishna would often give gifts to Radha… Kusum Sarovar… Radha Kund, Krishna Kund. He used to give. He was a king, wasn't he — he gave gifts of that level."

> "Merely saying it orally is a different thing. The king will say, 'I love you very much,' and later impose heavy taxes — no, it must show in action. He built roadways, built everything — **it is real love**."

> "He sat down with a japa mala and praised all day, 'honey, honey, honey, honey' — does that work?… **Praise alone is of no use; action is needed along with it.**"


## 3. The Beginning of Religion: Nature Worship and Ancestor Worship

Citing Swami Vivekananda, Acharya Ji turns to the history of religion:

> "Swami Vivekananda has described this very well in Bhakti Yoga, and in Karma Yoga as well."

The Brahmin class began thanking nature in their own ashrams — and Acharya Ji asks:

> "In their own huts, in their own ashrams, they started saying thank you, thank you, thank you, and doing the actions for it. **Was the intention bad?**"

> "They say, **religion started with ancestor worship and nature worship** — the worship of nature and the worship of the ancestors."

Ancestor worship was born of the same curiosity:

> "At some point the first man must have died… 'Arre, he was here yesterday, where did he go?' So he started thinking… then actions for him — then shraddha, pinda, everything must have started."

Even in today's shraddha both elements — praise and action — can be seen. And the worship of deities also began here, because:

> "The devatas started from this, because that is what could be seen. I first have to go **from form to the formless**, haven't I? It was said — whatever is seen, that alone will be worshipped."


## 4. The Birth of Dakshina: "You Will Have to Give Your Own Thanks"

Seeing the benefits the rishis were receiving, the Kshatriyas and Vaishyas grew curious:

> "Something is going on here, and good things are happening to them too — siddhis, vidya, something is coming to them, there is some benefit."

But the rajasic temperament had no time:

> "'I have no time; will you do it for us?'… The Vaishya also says, 'I have money; you do it, I'll give you dakshina.'"

The rishis' original answer is of great importance:

> "Arre, it doesn't work like that. I am giving thanks for what I have received from nature. **You will have to give your own thanks.** I can teach you that vidya, I can bring it down into you — from Surya to Manu, from Manu to Ikshvaku — *I may guide you, I may be your teacher*."

(*From Surya to Manu, from Manu to Ikshvaku* — an allusion to knowledge descending through the line of teacher and disciple.)

But the insistence grew, and in the end the Brahmin assembly accepted:

> "You're getting a cow for free, so take it — what does it cost you?"

Along with this, the knowledge of how to perform sadhana for someone else also developed:

> "Then how to do it while meditating on that person's mind — that is given in Patanjali… that by meditating on another, some exchange of my tapasya will take place."

For a while everyone was content:

> "The king was happy, the Vaishya was happy, all the others who served were happy too — at least we found someone to do puja for us. **Nobody learned it; only they knew.** Since then that karmakanda, karmakanda has been going on."


## 5. When Worship Became Mechanical

Generations changed, and the purpose was lost:

> "The Brahmin two or three generations later learned mechanically that their puja has to be performed. And the other learned that dakshina has to be given to the Brahmin."

> "**This one forgot for whom he was doing the puja; that one forgot why he was giving the dakshina.** Now both were mechanical — one performed mechanical puja, the other gave mechanical dakshina."

Then others copied the outer ritual as well:

> "He only offers this much in flowers, he does this, he does that — so I can do this too. **Not the atma-tattva part, not the purpose.**"

This is one of the central lessons of the chapter: when an action is separated from its **purpose**, it remains only mechanical ritual — which anyone can copy.


## 6. Many Deities, Many Pujas: The Veda Evolves Again

Seeing their knowledge copied, the Brahmins took a new direction:

> "Now this intellect had stagnated, and our business was gone too. **Now we have to evolve further.**"

> "Keeping just one deity like this won't work. Now we'll have many deities. Do you want a house? Then this puja. Do you want a car? Then this puja. Do you want a son? Then this puja."

But Acharya Ji does not see this as mere deception — he calls it a real evolution of the Veda:

> "**But it was not meant to trap anyone.** That was evolution — the Veda is evolving, understand? It is getting evolved."

> "Necessity is the mother of invention… I'm not saying this was done to trap people either — **the intellect genuinely evolved.**"

But history repeated itself:

> "Again four or five generations passed for both, and both forgot again… And at the end it became, like today, a **multi-billion dollar business**."


## 7. The Rebellion of Rishi Yajnavalkya

When the evolution of the Veda stopped again, one sage raised the question:

> "Rishi Yajnavalkya, being from a Brahmin family, did not like this. He says, '**What was the aim? Self-realisation.** For what did our ancestors start this?… This doesn't seem right to me. We will have to oppose it.'"

His guru cited tradition:

> "Even if it seems wrong, you will still have to do this, because this has now become the tradition. Now this has become a business, so it's business."

Yajnavalkya did not accept this, and his guru asked him to return the knowledge he had been given:

> "'If you don't accept it, then give back the vidya I gave you first, and then go.' It is said that he did **vamana**, that is, something like vomiting — symbolic of — giving back all that knowledge. 'Now I will discover something new of my own.'"

> "Then he immediately goes into meditation, and **he realised some higher truths** — all this is not in here; there is something a little higher than this."

But the established order opposed him. According to Acharya Ji, this is how the Yajurveda came to be divided:

> "They made a division — what the Brahmins do was given the name Shukla Yajurveda, and what he had was called black, all wrong — so Krishna Yajurveda."

Yajnavalkya was cast out of society — but his search did not stop:

> "And he had to go to some forest. He had to go to the jungle, to the *aranya*. **But he continued his research** — the knowing still went on."


## 8. Aranyaka and Upanishad: The Summit of the Veda

The knowledge received in the forest became the third part of the Veda:

> "Then whatever he came to understand, he gave back to society after some time — his time also had to come. **That part of the Veda is called Aranyaka.**"

(*Aranyaka* — the portion connected with the *aranya*, the forest.)

> "From that very Aranyaka arose the Upanishad named **Brihadaranyaka**, and the **Chandogya Upanishad**… which is a mixture of both — a little of the ritual section will come, the body will come, and the Self will come too."

And Acharya Ji ties the whole sequence of this journey into one sentence:

> "So first **Mantra Samhita, Brahmana, Aranyaka**, and beyond even this — on the basis of Rishi Yajnavalkya's knowledge — **the part by which one knew the Self became the Upanishad.** So this is the sequence."


## 9. The Message of the Gita: Rise Above the Three Gunas

Shri Krishna gives the conclusion of this whole journey of the Vedas in the Gita:

> **traiguṇya-viṣayā vedā nistraiguṇyo bhavārjuna,**
> **nirdvandvo nitya-sattva-stho niryoga-kṣema ātmavān.**
> "The Vedas deal with the subjects of the three gunas; O Arjuna, rise above the three gunas — free from the pairs of opposites, ever established in sattva, free from concern for acquiring and keeping, and established in the Self."
> — Bhagavad Gita 2.45

Acharya Ji opens up this verse word by word:

> "Go above all of this… **nirdvandvo** — dvandva means the pairs: cold and heat, gain and loss, victory and defeat, honour and dishonour — rise above these."

> "**nitya-sattva-stho** — so it says not to take up the actions of rajas and tamas… Where will you find sattva? **With a genuine guru.**"

> "**niryoga-kṣema** — give up even the worry about my yoga and kshema — what I am to get, what I am to keep. Then your guru will look after that."

(*Yoga* — acquiring what one does not have; *kshema* — protecting what one has.) And Shri Krishna repeats this assurance later:

> **ananyāś cintayanto māṁ ye janāḥ paryupāsate,**
> **teṣāṁ nityābhiyuktānāṁ yoga-kṣemaṁ vahāmy aham.**
> "Those who worship Me, thinking of Me with undivided devotion — for them, ever united with Me, I Myself carry what they need and preserve what they have."
> — Bhagavad Gita 9.22

> "'I will look after you.' So Krishna takes you there."


## 10. "Don't Read Me": The Unique Voice of the Veda

In the same context, pointing to the verses just before this one (Chapter 2), Acharya Ji says something startling:

> "**Veda is the only scripture in the world which shouts and tells, 'Don't read me.'**"

> "Now you call it the book of the Hindus, and Shri Krishna is our God, and the Gita is his guide — and he says not to read it… How shocked were you to hear that?"

> "The Veda — of Sanatan Dharma — from the mouth of Shri Krishna he says: **one who reads it is a fool; the deluded one reads the Veda.**"

But this does not mean abandoning the Veda:

> "But **one who reads Vedanta — that too is the Veda, isn't it?** Read only that much of the Veda."

> "Out of 100 pages, 96 pages were to be ignored, and you were to come straight to the last four pages. **That means one had to come to some guru and learn this final vidya.**"


## Summary: Key Teachings

1. **The Veda is an evolving journey** — Mantra Samhita, Brahmana, Aranyaka and Upanishad emerged one by one, along with humanity's growing understanding.
2. **The Mantra Samhita was born of gratitude** — the rishis saw the sincerity of nature, and *It is high time I should appreciate* — composition arose on its own from their praise.
3. **Love is expressed in action** — praise alone is not enough; from this understanding came the Brahmana portion, with yajna, dana, tapa and the methods of worship.
4. **Religion began with nature worship and ancestor worship** — whatever could be seen was worshipped; the journey from form to the formless.
5. **"You will have to give your own thanks"** — the rishis' original spirit was that every person should be grateful themselves; the guru is only the guide.
6. **Forgetting the purpose makes action mechanical** — both puja and dakshina became mechanical, and the atma-tattva was left behind.
7. **The courage of Rishi Yajnavalkya** — standing against tradition and the lobby, he continued the search for Self-realisation, from which the Aranyakas and Upanishads emerged.
8. **Nistraiguṇyo bhavārjuna** — rise above the three gunas and the pairs of opposites, stay established in sattva with a genuine guru, and leave the worry of yoga-kshema to the guru.
9. **The essence of the Veda is Vedanta** — leave 96 of the 100 pages and come to the last four, that is, learn that final vidya from a guru.
