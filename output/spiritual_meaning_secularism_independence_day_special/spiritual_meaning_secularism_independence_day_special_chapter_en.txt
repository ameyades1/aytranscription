# Chapter: The Spiritual Meaning of Secularism

## Hindustan and the Word "Secular"

We say that our country is secular. And yet, what is this land? Hindustan, the land of Sanatan Dharma. Still, what have we been made? Secular.

What does the word secular mean? In their view, you are free to practise any faith that you want. It is not only about dharma. If I believe in nothing at all, that too is my right in this country. I may believe in anything, and I may believe in nothing; that is also my right. I have the right to do whatever I wish here, as I please. One says I do not believe in God, another says I believe in God, another says I believe in a particular deity; I want to go here, I want to go there.

Let us not go too deep into this subject, but the issue must be understood, because it is connected to spirituality. Here it is the Guru speaking, not a king giving a speech. Everything has its own significance in our nation. So what is the spiritual idea behind secularism?

## One Truth, Many Voices

> **ekaṃ sad viprā bahudhā vadanti**
> "Truth is one; the wise speak of it in many ways."
> (Rig Veda 1.164.46)

Truth is one, and the wise describe it in many ways. Which truth? The truth of the Supreme Lord. Different scholars keep explaining it in different ways, by different methods. That is fine, as long as they are all describing that same one truth.

And if someone is teaching something wrong, he cannot be called a scholar at all; he simply is not one. But all the religions we see, all the paths we see, if they all speak of the one truth, whether it is a message of love or something else, then all is well.

## The Question Is How I Live My Own Dharma

The real point is how well I follow my own culture and my own faith. How my faith and my dharma will fare depends on this. The other person will follow his religion in his own way, or our nation says we must act according to secularism; abusing him, or hating him, brings no benefit. This is what the Guru says. Whether our country is secular or belongs to one religion is not the question right now.

> "If you can show the fruit of your faith, no one can break you."

This comes from practising your own culture and your own dharma. When you practise your own religion perfectly, no one can break you. Show your fruits. Do your sadhana. Show through your life all that is otherwise unseen.

## Atithi Devo Bhava, but with Discernment

People of all religions have kept coming and going in our nation. India was never against any particular religion. The very meaning of Sanatan Dharma is that we are open to all religions. We say:

> **atithidevo bhava**
> "Treat the guest as God."
> (Taittiriya Upanishad 1.11.2)

That is, regard the guest as a god. But if the guest walked straight into your bedroom, from tomorrow began deciding what is cooked in your kitchen, took over your wealth and finances directly, and then said that from now on you must live outside in a refugee area, whose fault is that? Yours. That is what we have usually done.

When people of other religions come, it does not mean that you keep anyone, anywhere, in any manner. It has been said that the rules for whoever comes from another religion or another country are not new; they are very old. I want to teach a little of Chanakya's, Kautilya's, Arthashastra and statecraft.

## Not Through Hatred, but Through Firmness in One's Own Dharma

No religion has ever grown great by hating others or by pointing out their faults. The essential point is how firm I can make my own dharma. And that is what "Antar" is all about.

## Show the Fruit of the Yamas

What do I always say? Yama, niyama, asana, pranayama, pratyahara, dharana, dhyana, samadhi.

> **yama-niyama-āsana-prāṇāyāma-pratyāhāra-dhāraṇā-dhyāna-samādhayo 'ṣṭāv aṅgāni**
> "Restraint, observance, posture, breath control, withdrawal of the senses, concentration, meditation and absorption are the eight limbs."
> (Patanjali Yoga Sutra 2.29)

This is the path to my liberation. But take only the first limb, yama. What does yama contain?

> **ahiṃsā-satya-asteya-brahmacarya-aparigrahā yamāḥ**
> "Non-violence, truthfulness, non-stealing, continence and non-possessiveness are the yamas."
> (Patanjali Yoga Sutra 2.30)

Truth, non-violence, non-stealing, brahmacharya and non-possessiveness. Just show the fruit of these five. Then say I am a Hindu, then say I am a yogi, then say that we believe in the Sutras of Patanjali.

> "One becomes a yogi by following Patanjali; you have to show its fruit."
