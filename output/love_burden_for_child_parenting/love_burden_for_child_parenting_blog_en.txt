# The Burden of Dreams and Rajasic Parenting

## Are You Raising Your Children This Way?

A mother remains a mother. One who raised children twenty years ago does not change her nature thirty or forty years later.

Today the subject is parenting from a **rajasic** disposition (driven by rajoguna, that is, by desire, ambition and the "I"). And "mother" here does not mean only the mother; fathers are included too.

As you read, check within yourself.

## Expectations Sitting on the Child's Head

What does a rajasic mother do to her children? Put all the pressure on them. A timetable, "work hard" pictures on the wall, school, this class, that activity. She sits constantly on the child's head.

When she says, "My son is the topper", it is the "I" that is speaking. The expectations are very high; they want some fruit. And if the child does not measure up, they become upset.

> "I could not do it, son; you must fulfil this dream."

The child's passion may lie elsewhere, yet by force they must carry their parents' unfinished dream.

*Do I want my child to achieve what I myself could not?*

## A Goat Led to the Butcher

With how much interest will such a child work? They see their death there.

Imagine a goat being dragged by a rope while the butcher says a knife is about to run across its neck. With how much enthusiasm will it go? With that same enthusiasm these children listen to their parents, because, being small, they can do nothing.

## The End of Rajoguna Is Sorrow

These same children leave home when they grow up. Searching outside for that same love, they either fall into immorality, or marry in the wrong place, or rebel and run away. They simply wait to grow up so they can be free.

> "The end of rajoguna is sorrow."

What the mother wanted, she mostly does not get. And even if she does, it comes in the wrong form.

## The Endless Race of Comparison

Without knowing whether the child has the capacity, classes are added: one, two, three.

And comparison all the time: "He got ahead in that exam; next time we will get ahead. He spoke at the function; at the next one we will show something different."

## Not Passion, but Money and Name

Rajasic parents push their children towards **material success** and fame, which leads to stress and anxiety in children.

"You must come first." "Become an engineer; it pays the most." "Do an MBA." A profession is chosen not because the child has a passion for it, but because it brings money, name and fame.

*Do my choices for my child come from their passion, or from my ambition?*

## A Childhood Dressed Up for Photos

Rajasic mothers want the child to look perfect on social media and in public. A **sattvic** (pure and natural) mother says, "Let them be natural; it is a small child." A little decent and presentable, that is fine.

> "A child is a child; let the child be like a child."

But here there is full makeup, like film heroes and heroines, because the child has to be in photos and go to functions. Even real heroines do not do this much. Children of eight or nine are made to dance to love songs whose words they do not even understand.

There is nothing wrong with dressing children up. The question is what fills the home. Where there is much of the world and materialism and little of God, the child absorbs, from childhood, the dialogues, dances and clothes of TV serials.

## Key Takeaways

- A parent's nature does not change with time, so it is necessary to examine one's own disposition.
- Rajasic parenting runs on the "I": high expectations, pressure and the burden of unfinished dreams.
- A child crushed under this burden leaves home when grown and seeks love outside, in the wrong places.
- The end of rajoguna is sorrow; what was wanted is mostly not obtained.
- Comparison, many classes and choosing a profession for money bring stress and anxiety to children.
- Let a child be a child; let the home have more of God and less of show.
