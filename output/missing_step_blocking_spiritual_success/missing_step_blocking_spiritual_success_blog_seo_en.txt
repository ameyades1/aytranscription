# The Missing Step That Is Holding Back Your Sadhana

You have listened to discourses and read the scriptures, and perhaps a Guru has even taken you through the Bhagavad Gita. Why, then, does nothing change within you? Acharya Upendra Ji answers with an ancient sequence: **shravana, manana and nididhyasana**.

These are three practices every seeker on the path of **sadhana** (spiritual practice) has to go through. Too often we leave one of them half done and leap ahead to the next.

In short, shravana manana nididhyasana is the three-step sequence of sadhana. Shravana (listening) is complete when you can repeat the Guru's teaching point by point; manana (reflection) is complete when no doubt remains; and nididhyasana turns belief into experience, which demands tapasya and qualification. Leave any one step half done, and your sadhana is held back.

## Shruti: What Was Received by Listening

The first practice is **shravana** (listening). The word **shruti** (that which is heard) comes from the same root, and our Vedas are called shruti.

Notice that reading and writing play no part here. The Guru spoke, and the disciple took it in by listening. That is why the Vedas came to be known as shruti.

In the **sankalpa** (the formal statement of intent recited before worship), we say:

> **śruti-smṛti-purāṇokta-phala-prāptyartham**
> "For attaining the fruit described in the Shruti, the Smriti and the Puranas."
> (From the sankalpa recited before puja)

**Smriti** (remembered tradition) is what has already been held in memory, such as the Manusmriti. Rules and laws grow out of events that happened before. A **Purana** (ancient lore) tells of what existed long ago and still applies today.

## When Is Shravana Complete?

Here Acharya Ji asks a direct question: when is listening complete?

Suppose a Guru explained the Gita to you. Did every one of the hundred points settle in your mind the first time? If you absorbed only ten percent, your listening was only ten percent complete as well.

This is why the scriptures tell you to do **parayana** (a full reading of a sacred text from beginning to end) eleven times, or seven times. Each time you read the same text, your consciousness climbs one step higher.

If you have not finished listening, Acharya Ji asks, what exactly will you reflect on?

When, then, is listening complete? It is complete when you can repeat your Guru's teaching point by point, or word for word. Even if the exact words escape you, it is complete when the essence of what the Guru meant has become one hundred percent your own.

If that takes eleven readings, do eleven. If it takes a hundred, do a hundred.

*Could you explain, point by point, the text or teaching you say you "know"?*

## Manana: Moving Past "I Just Can't Believe It"

Suppose you can now repeat the Guru's words exactly. A new problem appears at once: "Guruji, I know what you are trying to say, but I cannot bring myself to believe it."

Does this practice really bear so much fruit? Does doing this truly bring about that result?

This is where you need **manana** (reflection). Reflection means:

- Reading other texts and visiting libraries
- Looking at what others have experienced and the results they received
- Taking your doubts to the Guru

When does reflection come to an end? It ends when no doubt remains, when you can say, "Yes, this practice brings this result. Lord Ganesha is exactly as described. I have no more doubts."

## Nididhyasana: The Step People Most Often Skip

Then comes the third problem: "I believe what you say, but I do not experience it."

The Swami had the experience, that great soul had it, that king had it. How will it happen for me?

This is where **nididhyasana** (deep, sustained practice that brings what you have learned into lived experience) begins. You take what you have understood and accepted and make it part of your life. The question is a plain one: can you do what those who reached that experience actually did?

## "We Are Intellectuals": The Doctors' Question

When Acharya Ji began teaching methods of healing, some doctors came to him. "We are intellectuals," they said. "Is this some kind of miracle, or is there a process that we could repeat too?"

Acharya Ji answered: yes, of course. You can practise it as well. But you too will have to undergo the same **tapasya** (intense spiritual discipline) and earn the qualification for it.

Anyone can practise medicine, but only once they have earned their MBBS, the basic medical degree. Nobody gets to say, "I will practise however I like."

In the same way, the doctors would have to undertake the kind of tapasya Acharya Ji did and become qualified in it. Only then would they be able to heal as well.

*Are you expecting results from your sadhana that you have not yet qualified for?*

## Is Your Experience Real or Imagined?

During nididhyasana, experiences begin to arise. Not every one of them is real, though. It may be a hallucination, a picture conjured by your own mind.

Some people see light the moment they close their eyes, some see God, and some notice a fragrance. Acharya Ji says that none of this amounts to anything; such sensations are called **tanmatra** (subtle sense impressions).

How, then, can you know an experience is genuine? Acharya Ji gives three tests:

1. **Proof in the scriptures:** The scriptures of the tradition your Guru follows should describe that experience.
2. **Agreement with earlier masters:** Gurus established in **samadhi** (the deepest state of meditative absorption), or the ancient rishis and sages, should have reached that state before you, and your experience should match theirs.
3. **Confirmation from a living Guru:** Your living Guru should verify it.

Only when all three are present can you accept your experience as true.

Whether it is a small healing, a great spiritual power or even **mukti** (liberation) itself, these same three tests decide what is genuine.

## Key Takeaways

- Sadhana involves three practices, **shravana, manana and nididhyasana**, and their order matters.
- Listening is complete when you can repeat the Guru's teaching point by point or word for word, which takes repeated parayana.
- Reflection is complete when no doubt remains; study, the experiences of others and questions to the Guru all help.
- Nididhyasana turns belief into experience, but it demands tapasya and qualification, just as a doctor needs a degree.
- Experiences of light, visions or fragrance may be tanmatra or the mind's own imagination.
- An experience is genuine only when it passes all three tests: the scriptures, earlier masters established in samadhi, and a living Guru.
