# Whatever Comes Must Also Go: Overcoming Life's Struggles with the Gita

The doctor said, "There is no cure for this." Fear took hold of us, and we spent the rest of our lives sitting with that illness.

Sometimes a relationship breaks, or we quarrel with someone, and we carry that quarrel for lifetimes. Sometimes it is a shortage of money, poverty or the lack of a job, and a whole life slips by in the same state.

The real question, then, is this: **why does the situation never change?**

## What Is the Gita's First Teaching?

To answer this question, we need to open the Bhagavad Gita at the second chapter, fourteenth verse. This is where Shri Krishna gives Arjuna his very first teaching.

He tells Arjuna that his present situation must have come partly from **prarabdha** (destiny, the fruit of past actions), partly from the actions he has done, and partly from his **samskaras** (the deep impressions left on the mind).

> **mātrāsparśās tu kaunteya**
> **śītoṣṇasukhaduḥkhadāḥ**
> **āgamāpāyino 'nityās**
> **tāṁs titikṣasva bhārata**
> "O son of Kunti, the contact of the senses with their objects brings cold and heat, pleasure and pain. These come and go; they are impermanent. Bear them patiently, O Bharata."
> (Bhagavad Gita 2.14)

## Dvandva: The Pairs of Opposites in Life

Cold and heat are **dvandva** (pairs of opposites) that you meet at the level of the body. Pleasure and pain are the pairs you meet at the level of the mind.

We call them favourable and unfavourable circumstances. These ups and downs will keep coming as long as you live.

Where do they come from? They arise when our senses meet the objects of the outside world. Because you have a tongue, there is food, whether idli-sambhar or anything else. Because you have ears, there is music, there are words. Sound, touch, form, relationships and the five great elements all come into contact with us, and that contact gives birth to either pleasure or pain.

Shri Krishna, however, says two things about them: they are born and they perish, and they are **anitya** (impermanent).

## Then How Can an Illness Be Permanent?

Here the Guru raises a direct question. How can a doctor declare that cancer is permanent, or that once you have diabetes, you will live with it for life, when Shri Krishna himself says such conditions are impermanent?

Acharya Ji says that nothing is permanent; change alone is constant, and change is bound to happen. The same holds true for poverty. Whatever situation has arisen must also come to an end. You are not the one who must end; the situation must.

*Which situation in your life have you accepted as "permanent" and learned to live with?*

Keep one thing in mind, though: that situation will not leave by itself. You have to do something about it.

## Endurance: The First Step, Not the Destination

Shri Krishna's first teaching is **titiksha** (patient endurance). But is this his final word?

Look closely. In the first chapter, only Arjuna speaks. In the second chapter too, Arjuna does all the talking up to the tenth verse. Shri Krishna begins speaking at the eleventh verse, and this first teaching arrives at the fourteenth.

The trouble is that most of us cannot even endure properly. Besides, Shri Krishna never said, "Spend your whole life just putting up with it." After endurance comes an entire journey that runs all the way to the eighteenth chapter.

## The Journey Through the Gita's Eighteen Chapters

Beyond endurance, the Gita lays out a complete process:

- **Chapter Three, Karma Yoga:** You must take some new action. Not action alone, but yoga, which means you must join something to it.
- **Chapter Four, Yajna:** The word **yajna** (sacred offering) comes from the root "yaj", which means to sacrifice. It is far more than an outer ritual.
- **Chapter Five:** The teaching on devotion.
- **Chapter Six:** How to practise sadhana, and the state of meditation.
- **Chapters Seven to Twelve:** The process of realising "how I myself am that **Brahman** (the ultimate reality)"; the eleventh chapter describes the Lord's cosmic form.
- **Chapters Thirteen to Eighteen:** How all of this comes about, and what the three **gunas** (the qualities of nature) are.

In this way, the Gita explains all four yogas: action, devotion, knowledge and meditation.

*If the first teaching were everything, why would Shri Krishna take Arjuna through such a long process?*

## Key Takeaways

- No hardship is permanent, whether it is an illness, a broken relationship or poverty.
- Pleasure and pain arise from the contact of the senses with their objects, and they are impermanent (Gita 2.14).
- Whatever situation has arisen must come to an end; not you, but the situation.
- The situation will not change by itself; you have to act.
- Endurance is the Gita's first teaching, not its last.
- After it comes the full journey of action, sacrifice, devotion, meditation and knowledge of Brahman.
