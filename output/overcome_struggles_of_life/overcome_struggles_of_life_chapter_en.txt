# Chapter: From Endurance to Transformation

## Are Life's Hard Situations Permanent?

When great doctors call an illness incurable, what do they say? They say, "There is no remedy for this." And we too become afraid. Then we sit with that illness for the whole of our life.

Or some relationship goes bad, there is a quarrel with someone, and we carry that quarrel for lifetimes. Or look at one's financial situation: money, wealth, someone's poverty, some lack, having no job. People spend their entire life just like that.

So the question is: why does this situation not change?

## The First Teaching of the Bhagavad Gita

For this, we will need to read a little of the Bhagavad Gita. Turn to Chapter Two, verse fourteen. Shri Krishna gave his first teaching in the Bhagavad Gita in the fourteenth verse of the second chapter. He says, the situation you are in has come partly through *prarabdha* (destiny born of past actions); there must have been such karmas, there must have been such *samskaras* (impressions).

> **mātrā-sparśās tu kaunteya śītoṣṇa-sukha-duḥkha-dāḥ**
> **āgamāpāyino 'nityās tāṁs titikṣasva bhārata**
> "O son of Kunti, the contacts of the senses with their objects give rise to cold and heat, pleasure and pain. They come and go and are impermanent; endure them, O Bharata."
> (Bhagavad Gita 2.14)

Shri Krishna teaches: O son of Kunti, cold and heat, that is, the **dvandva** (pairs of opposites) that come at the level of the body. Cold and heat, pleasure and pain; pleasure and pain are spoken of here as states of the mind. These opposites will keep coming. They are called favourable and unfavourable circumstances. In life, ups and downs will keep coming.

What gives rise to them is the contact of the senses, our sense organs, with their objects. And these contacts are **subject to arising and perishing**, and **impermanent**.

## The Contact of the Senses and Their Objects

This is the contact of your body, your mind, with the objects of the outer world. If there is a tongue, there is some food, whether idli-sambar or anything else. If there are ears, there is a great deal of music or some word to hear. Sound, touch, form, everything is there.

When all this comes into contact, relationships come, the five great elements come, and from that contact something arises. What arises? Either pleasure or pain. Fine, but what is it like? Shri Krishna says it is perishable and impermanent.

## How Can Illness and Poverty Be Permanent?

So how does your illness become permanent? My question is, how can a doctor say that cancer is permanent, or that once diabetes comes it will never go, that now it will stay for life? When Shri Krishna himself says it is impermanent.

> "Nothing is permanent; the only thing that is constant is change."

And change must happen. How does my poverty become permanent? In truth it should be impermanent; it should go after some days. Shri Krishna called it perishable, which means the situation has arisen; it has come into being, and now it should perish.

> "Not you, but that situation should be destroyed."

That means poverty too should go after some days. But it will not go on its own; something has to be done for it.

## Endurance: The First Teaching, Not the Last

That is why Shri Krishna says, O Bharata, endure them. So what was the very first teaching? Endure. But is this the final teaching?

This is the second chapter. In the first chapter, only Arjuna speaks. In the second chapter too, up to the tenth verse, only Arjuna speaks. Shri Krishna's words begin from the eleventh verse, and this is the first thing said, in the fourteenth verse itself. The problem is that a person cannot even endure properly.

Shri Krishna did not say, "Endure, and keep enduring until your whole life is spent." After enduring, one has to go on to the third chapter, the fourth, fifth, sixth, seventh, and so on up to the eighteenth. Does one have to go or not?

## The Whole Journey of the Gita

In the third chapter he speaks of Karma Yoga: you will have to do some new action. Not only action, but yoga; something has to be joined. What yoga is, we will read all of that in two minutes.

In the fourth chapter he explained how yajna is performed. What does yajna mean, only an outer ritual? Yesterday we also heard the definition of yajna: the word yajna comes from the root **"yaj"**, and yaj means to sacrifice, to renounce.

Then in the fifth chapter he will speak about bhakti. In the sixth chapter, how sadhana is to be done, that is, the state of meditation. In this way he explains the four yogas: karma, bhakti, jnana and dhyana.

From the seventh to the twelfth chapter, he gives the process of knowing "how I myself am that Brahman." The eleventh chapter describes the entire cosmic form. From the thirteenth to the eighteenth chapter, he gives the whole process: what it is, how it all comes about, and what the three gunas are.

So what do you think, why did Shri Krishna tell Arjuna such a long process?
