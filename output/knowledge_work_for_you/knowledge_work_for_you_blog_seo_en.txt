# Why Can't We Do What Is Right, Even When We Know It?

Have you ever known what was right and still failed to do it? Have you ever known something was wrong and still been unable to let it go?

You are not alone in this struggle. Thousands of years ago, Duryodhana said exactly the same thing. Through two verses from the third chapter of the Bhagavad Gita, Acharya Upendra Ji explains where this deeply human problem comes from and how to resolve it.

Why do we end up knowing what is right but not doing it? Bhagavad Gita 3.33 says that even the learned act according to their own nature, so book learning alone does not stop wrongdoing. Gita 3.34 gives the way out: do not fall under the control of attraction and aversion. Acharya Ji's advice: choose shreyas over preyas.

## Every Being Is Bound by Its Nature

> **sadṛśaṁ ceṣṭate svasyāḥ prakṛter jñānavān api**
> **prakṛtiṁ yānti bhūtāni nigrahaḥ kiṁ kariṣyati**
> "Even the learned act in keeping with their own nature. All beings follow their nature, so what can forceful restraint achieve?"
> (Bhagavad Gita 3.33)

A "being" here does not mean only dogs and cats. Anything that carries **prana** (the life force) within it is a being, and that includes every one of us.

All beings live according to their **prakriti** (inborn nature), acting under the sway of their **svabhava** (temperament). "Sva" means oneself, and "bhava" means one's feelings. What the verse is really describing, then, is the mind.

No two fingers on a hand are the same. Each person speaks in their own way, behaves in their own way and works through their own qualities. None of this should surprise us.

## Does Knowledge Alone Stop Wrongdoing?

The verse goes on to say that even the learned act according to their nature.

The learning meant here is not **tattvajnana** (realisation of the ultimate truth), which the Gita takes up in its eighteenth chapter. This is ordinary learning. Someone may have read many books, studied the scriptures, memorised mantras, practised **sadhana** (spiritual discipline) and even become a respected pandit. Still, they act as their nature dictates. What, then, can anyone's stubborn effort to hold them back achieve?

Acharya Ji points out that knowledge alone does not stop a person from doing wrong.

*Have you ever wondered why, after all you have read and heard, your behaviour has not changed?*

## The Gita Was First Offered to Duryodhana

Few people know that Shri Krishna tried to counsel Duryodhana and Dhritarashtra before he ever spoke to Arjuna. They did not listen.

The rule is to teach the scriptures to a wrongdoer first, so that he never commits the wrong at all. If there is no wrongdoing, there is no war. Yet a wicked person does not stop, and Duryodhana did not stop either.

This is why a good person must also learn how to strike back when the need arises. The second chapter of the Gita spoke of endurance. From that point on, the Lord no longer says only "endure"; at some point you have to resist.

## "Don't Lecture Me"

When Shri Krishna went to reason with Duryodhana, the reply was: don't lecture me. It is the same thing young people say today: "Spare us the lecture, we already know everything."

Did Duryodhana lack knowledge? He had studied in the same **gurukul** (a traditional school where students live with their teacher) as Arjuna. Ignorance was not his problem. He admitted it himself: he knew what **dharma** (righteous duty) was, yet felt no wish to follow it, and he knew what was wrong, yet could not give it up.

Then he asked Shri Krishna to tell him if there was any cure for this.

We face the same problem. A Duryodhana and a Dhritarashtra sit hidden inside each of us.

## The Mahabharata Within Us

**Dhritarashtra** is blind. He stands for the **mind**, which shapes our temperament. The mind truly is blind, because it gets trapped in **maya** (illusion).

**Gandhari** could see. She stands for the **intellect**. The intellect should rank above the mind and show it the way. Yet Gandhari tied a blindfold over her eyes, when she should have spent her life guiding her blind husband.

When we live entirely at the mercy of the mind and its habits, we blindfold our intellect too, and we do it knowingly.

## A Hundred Kauravas, a Hundred Distortions

The result is the **Kauravas**, the hundred brothers. We have ten senses: five **karmendriyas** (organs of action) and five **jnanendriyas** (organs of perception). Each carries ten distortions, and ten times ten gives a hundred.

**Duryodhana** is the one who stands far from true wealth. That wealth is not money but **daivi sampatti** (divine qualities): compassion, forgiveness and love. **Dushasana** is the one who cannot govern himself.

These are not people out in the world. They are tendencies inside us.

This is the source of trouble in so many lives: people know what is right but have no will to do it. Even after going to the Guru, they fail to do what they know they should.

*Which is stronger in you: the blind mind or the awakened intellect?*

## The Way Out: Rising Above Likes and Dislikes

Blinded by **moha** (attachment), Duryodhana and Dhritarashtra missed what Arjuna grasped. The answer comes in the very next verse:

> **indriyasyendriyasyārthe rāga-dveṣau vyavasthitau**
> **tayor na vaśam āgacchet tau hy asya paripanthinau**
> "Attraction and aversion lie hidden in every sense's response to its objects. Do not fall under their control, for both are enemies on your path."
> (Bhagavad Gita 3.34)

Whatever object, person or place we connect with in this world, every sense holds a hidden **raga** (attraction, liking) and **dvesha** (aversion, dislike) toward it.

You like this food and not that one. When it comes to marriage, you favour this person and not that one. Everyone's likes and dislikes are different.

The Gita says you must not let either of them rule you. Do not do something only because you like it, and do not avoid something only because you dislike it. Attraction and aversion are both great enemies on the path to your highest good.

## Shreyas or Preyas?

What should you do, then? Do what the Guru tells you is right.

The scriptures call these two choices **shreyas** (what is truly good) and **preyas** (what is pleasant). Shreyas is the higher path, the one the Guru points out. Preyas is whatever feels pleasing to me. "I enjoy it" is no reason to keep doing something.

Acharya Ji says the scriptures themselves should become what you love.

*Of the decisions you made today, how many came from shreyas and how many from preyas?*

## Key Takeaways

- All beings, the learned included, act according to their nature; book learning alone does not stop wrongdoing.
- Duryodhana knew right from wrong, but lacked the will to do right and the strength to give up wrong.
- Dhritarashtra symbolises the blind mind, Gandhari the intellect, and the hundred Kauravas the hundred distortions within us.
- Duryodhana is the one far from divine wealth (compassion, forgiveness, love); all these figures are tendencies inside us.
- The answer lies in Gita 3.34: do not fall under the sway of attraction and aversion, the enemies on the path to your highest good.
- Choose shreyas over preyas: do what the Guru teaches, and learn to love the scriptures.
