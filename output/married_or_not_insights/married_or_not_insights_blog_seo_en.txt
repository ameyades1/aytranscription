# The True Meaning of Pativrata: Living with Radiance, Not in Submission

Does being a **pativrata** (a wife wholly devoted to her husband) mean silently enduring everything? Many women carry this question in their hearts. It came up again and again, in private messages and during **sadhana** (spiritual practice).

In this discourse, Acharya Ji answers it through the lens of Karma Yoga and the scriptures. His answer is gentle, and it is also clear.

The short answer is no. Here is the pativrata meaning the scriptures give: she is a form of the Devi who carries tej. So she speaks up for herself, says "no" with confidence and courtesy, fulfils both her interests and her duties, and never tolerates abuse.

## Pativrata: A Form of the Goddess

The scriptures describe a pativrata as a form of the Devi, the Divine Mother. She carries **tej** (spiritual radiance and strength) within her. It is said that in her presence even a demon would turn into Govinda, a name of Shri Krishna.

In other words, she has the power to transform someone into a reflection of God. But if she herself lives crushed and silenced, where has her divine strength gone?

Strength is life; weakness is death. A pativrata must therefore also know how to speak in her own voice.

## The Power to Transform, and the Courage of Mahishasuramardini

The daughter of the saint Eknath Maharaj was married to the wrong man. Eknath Maharaj told her to teach her husband one verse of the Bhagavad Gita every day. Slowly, the man changed for the better.

Transformation is possible. Yet becoming divine also means becoming **Mahishasuramardini** (Devi Durga as the slayer of the buffalo demon Mahishasura) when the moment calls for it. You must have the strength to bring down the other person's ego.

## "What Will People Say?"

Swami Vivekananda says that if you are not living a certain way, then you are not who you believe yourself to be.

A thought arises that this relationship should end, but fear of society holds you back: "I am staying only for the little ones. Otherwise I would have left long ago."

Acharya Ji says the fault here is yours, not theirs.

Such a life holds neither bliss nor energy. In Acharya Ji's view, this is not the life of a pativrata.

*Are you holding back from a decision only because of what people might say?*

## She Has the Power to Speak

A thousand years of living under suppression have left Indian women hesitant: "Should I say yes or no? If I say yes, it will become an issue at home."

The truth is that she has that power. She can speak. And when she stays silent, then in Vivekananda's words, she is not who she claims to be.

## The First Two Years: Show Who You Really Are

The first two years of a marriage shape an image. We often present a false one, claiming we do this much and that much, and then we have to carry that image for the rest of our lives.

If anything shifts even slightly in the third year, people ask, "What has come over her now?"

So show your real self from the very beginning. **Learn to say "no" with confidence.** It is enough to say, "I like this, and I do not like that."

Say it without harshness, though. Restraint in **speech** matters most of all.

## Do Not Kill Your Own Wishes

The Vishnu Purana describes **grihastha dharma** (the duties of household life) in detail. Its message is that even within marriage, a woman need not live by crushing her own wishes.

Perhaps she wants to take a course, study part time, or join a class she loves, and her mother-in-law brings up the housework. She should then put her case across in a sweet, gentle voice.

Acharya Ji explains that leaving your own interests unfulfilled leads to rebirth, and so does leaving your duties undone.

The way through is simple: "Mother, I will definitely take care of all this when I get back from class." Or: "I will finish this work and then go for an hour or two." She keeps her word and pursues her interest too. She should have at least this much courage.

## When Courage Falls Short

Then she should know how to talk to her husband. The scriptures also speak of setting up a separate household, meaning living apart for a while, without breaking the relationship.

This is not "I will go back to my parents' home." It is "I will go, and take my husband with me." A mother-in-law's mind is often caught between authority over her daughter-in-law and control over her son. When husband and wife go together, a short period of distance works.

All of this rests on one condition: the woman must have **tej**, and she must keep up her **sadhana**.

## The Last Resort: Liberation Comes First

What Meerabai's Guru told her is the last resort. **Liberation** (mukti, freedom from the cycle of birth and death) is the ultimate goal, not the husband.

**Pativrata dharma** (the duty of a devoted wife) is one part of life, not the whole of it. What you do should carry you towards liberation. Only then are you a true **karma yogi** (one who walks the path of selfless action).

> **jāke priya na rāma baidehī |**
> **tajie tāhi koṭi bairī sama, jadyapi parama sanehī ||**
> "Whoever does not hold Rama and Vaidehi (Sita) dear, abandon them as you would ten million enemies, even if they are your most beloved."
> (Goswami Tulsidas, Vinaya Patrika, Pada 174)

Here, those who do not hold Rama and Sita dear are read as anyone who blocks the path to liberation, whether a relative or anyone else. That includes even a husband who says, "You are not going to **satsang** (a gathering for spiritual teaching) tomorrow."

Acharya Ji says the ultimate goal is the path of liberation; the husband is not the ultimate goal.

Remember, though, that this is the **last resort**, never the first.

## Never Tolerate Abuse

If a daughter-in-law is abused, branded or tortured, as still happens in villages today and sometimes in cities too, she must not put up with it.

If it stays within limits, she should respond at once herself. If it crosses those limits, she should call her parents, and firm action must follow.

## A Mother's Invisible Thread

Acharya Ji always says that when trouble brews in a daughter's home, her own mother is often responsible.

An invisible bond, called the **silver cord**, links a mother and her child. The physical umbilical cord is cut, but this bond remains. Every thought and every worry of the mother affects her daughter immediately. The more she worries, the faster it reaches her.

Mothers, too, must act responsibly.

## Why Complain About Everything?

Letters used to take days; now messages fly every minute. A daughter reports what happened today but never tells the story of the past fifteen days: "I wanted red curtains and they told me to buy black ones. They are **tamasic** (dull and heavy by nature), and I am **rajasic** (lively and passionate)!"

Hearing half the story, the mother concludes that her daughter is having a terrible time. Then comes the advice: "You should tell them the same thing." This is where the whole relationship can start to fall apart.

The mother means well, but **attachment** keeps her from seeing fairly. Half-formed understanding leads to wrong guidance.

*Are your conversations holding your home together, or quietly pulling it apart?*

## How Often Should a Married Daughter Call Home?

A true pativrata says, "This is my home now. **Kanyadaan** (the ritual in which parents give their daughter away in marriage) has taken place." Her surname and her middle name have both changed.

Today phone calls, video calls and photos flow constantly. According to the scriptures, calling your parents' home every minute is not a healthy sign. It raises a question: does she live in this house, or in that one?

When should you get in touch? When there is a real problem, or when things are getting out of control. When there is a family function or some good news to share. But not three calls a day.

Acharya Ji says his role is to share what the scriptures teach, and too much contact brings trouble into married life.

If you have moved abroad, make friends there and live the life that place offers.

## The Same Applies to Sons

A son moves to America, and his mother keeps calling: "Have you eaten?" One day, fed up, he will say, "I can take care of myself."

If you have sent him away, let him live on his own for a while. Otherwise you should have kept him close. All of us need to be very careful here.

## Key Takeaways

- A pativrata lives with tej and sadhana, not in submission; she must know how to speak in her own voice.
- Show your real self in the first two years of marriage, and learn to say "no" with confidence and courtesy.
- Fulfil both your interests and your duties; leaving either undone leads to rebirth.
- If needed, living apart for a while together with your husband is a remedy, not a break-up.
- Liberation is the ultimate goal; leaving someone who blocks it is the last resort, never the first.
- Never tolerate abuse; if it crosses limits, call your parents and take firm action.
- Constant contact with your parents' home and complaining about everything harm married life; mothers must also act responsibly.
