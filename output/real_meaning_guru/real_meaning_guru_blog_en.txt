# The Secret of Guru Purnima and the Real Meaning of Vyasa

Every year we celebrate **Guru Purnima** (the full-moon festival honouring the Guru). But have you ever stopped to ask why it falls on a full moon? And what was the real name of Maharshi Vedavyasa, the sage we remember on this day?

Let us uncover the meaning hidden behind this festival.

## Why Not the New Moon?

Everything we do for our ancestors, we do on **Amavasya** (the new-moon day). Why, then, do we honour the Guru on **Purnima** (the full-moon day)?

It is a fundamental question. The answer lies in what the sun and the moon stand for.

## The Sun Is the Self, the Moon Is the Mind

**The sun symbolises the Atman (the Self).** The Atman is the "I", my own true self. I am not this body.

**The moon symbolises the mind**, the **jivatma** (the individual soul).

Now picture the full light of that Self, of God Himself, reflected on the moon. That is the full moon. Whose state of mind could this be? Acharya Ji explains that it is the state of a Guru.

The Guru is the one whose mind reflects the light of the Self completely. That is why the Guru's festival always falls on the full moon.

*How much of that inner light does your own mind reflect today: half, a little, or none at all?*

## Vyasa Was Never a Name

This day is also called **Vyasa Purnima**. Most people assume Vyasa was the sage's name, but it was not.

His name was **Krishna Dvaipayana**. "Vedavyasa" is his title.

**Veda** means knowing, the process of coming to know. **Vyasa** means dividing, breaking something into parts.

## Why Did Someone Divide the Vedas?

In that age, a student's education was not complete until they had memorised everything. Think about it: could anyone hold so vast a body of wisdom in memory, in full detail?

So the Vedas, Puranas, scriptures and Upanishads, all the knowledge there was, were divided into smaller branches. Whoever carried out this division of the Vedas came to be called Vyasa. As Acharya Ji explains, Vyasa is a position, not a personal name.

Krishna Dvaipayana, in whose honour we celebrate Guru Purnima, is the **twenty-eighth Vyasa**.

## Seventeen Puranas, Yet No Peace

Maharshi Vedavyasa travelled the length and breadth of India. He studied its many cultures and traditions, and the different paths of devotion, knowledge and action. Then he composed seventeen Puranas.

They held a great deal of **tattva jnana** (essential spiritual knowledge). Even so, he could not sleep. He was not at peace.

*Have you ever felt that, despite all you know, something inside you still feels incomplete?*

## The Bhagavata Purana: Where the Journey Ended

His work finally felt complete when he wrote the eighteenth, the **Bhagavata Purana**. At its heart, it speaks of **bhakti** (devotion).

His message was clear: knowledge without devotion has no meaning, and you must become a devotee who also knows.

That is exactly who we, too, are meant to become.

## Key Takeaways

- Guru Purnima falls on the full moon because the sun's full light (the Atman) shining on the moon (the mind) symbolises the state of the Guru.
- Rites for the ancestors take place on the new moon; the Guru's festival takes place on the full moon.
- Vedavyasa's real name was Krishna Dvaipayana. Vyasa is a position, meaning "the one who divides".
- Krishna Dvaipayana is the twenty-eighth Vyasa.
- Seventeen Puranas full of knowledge brought no contentment; the devotion of the eighteenth, the Bhagavata Purana, completed his work.
- Let knowledge and devotion walk together: the goal is to become a devotee who knows.
