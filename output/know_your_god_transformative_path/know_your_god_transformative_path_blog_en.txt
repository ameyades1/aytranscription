# Know God, Don't Just Believe

Have you ever wondered how well you really know the God you worship every day? We bow our heads before an image of Ganapati, Lord Shiva or the Devi, but is that bond one of love, or is it habit and need?

In this discourse, Acharya Ji raises that very question through a simple example that stings.

## Why Was God Given a Form?

Maharshi Vedavyasa says:

> **sādhakānāṁ hitārthāya brahmaṇo rūpakalpanā |**
> "For the good of seekers, a form has been imagined for Brahman (the Supreme Reality)."
> (Traditional saying; compare Ramapurvatapaniya Upanishad 1.7)

God is **nirakara** (formless), and the formless is hard to grasp. That is why we were given a **sakara** (with form) image, so that we could lean on it and reach the true God beyond it.

The form is a ladder, not the destination. Acharya Ji is clear that if you lose yourself in the form and stop there, it will not do.

## Can You Love a Stranger's Photograph?

Imagine you come across a stranger's photograph in a newspaper. You do not know this person and would not recognise them; you have no idea where they live. And yet you announce, "I have fallen in love."

A picture in a magazine can stir attraction, but love? Now suppose you go further, declare that your marriage has been arranged, and tell the photograph every day, "I love you." What would people say? They would think you had lost your mind.

As Acharya Ji puts it, you do not even know this person, would not even recognise them, and still you claim, "I am bound to them; I have fallen in love."

He points out that our religion is slowly taking on the same shape. The photograph has become an idol, a three-dimensional figure like those in a wax museum. Yet we do not know the One we are worshipping.

## Is Your Devotion Built on Love or on Self-Interest?

The first step of **bhakti** (devotion) is love. People connect with someone either out of love or out of self-interest.

Now look honestly. What do our prayers usually sound like? "O Lord, give me this." God gives, gets our work done and clears the obstacles from our path, and that is why we stay connected.

*How much love has gone into your prayers so far, and how much asking?*

If the love is real, the wish to know Him will awaken on its own. Otherwise it is the love of blind faith.

## Know Him, Don't Just Accept Him

The Bhagavad Gita and the Ganesha Gita (Lord Ganesha's teaching to King Varenya, from the Ganesha Purana) say the same thing: you must **know** God, not merely **believe** in Him. Acharya Ji makes it plain that the scriptures speak of knowing God, not of taking Him on faith.

We accept Ganesha as though someone had imposed His form on us. Acharya Ji says our own rishis call this a mistake. Worshipping this way is like marrying a photograph.

And consider this: somewhere there may be a true devotee who has actually attained Ganesha. It is as if the woman in the photograph had already married someone else while you sat clutching her picture. Acharya Ji is quick to add that this is only an analogy: a wife belongs to one husband, but Ganapati can belong to thousands.

## The Questions You Should Be Asking

The journey to God begins with questions:

- Why does Ganapati have an elephant's face?
- If, as the popular saying goes, "there is one Master of all", how did He come to have so many forms?
- If there is one Master of all, why is Ganapati absent from other religions?
- Is my God confined to my religion?

*Have you ever asked "why" about the deity you hold dearest?*

## Saying "Shankar" Is Forbidden!

During Diwali, Acharya Ji visited the home of an acquaintance. Among the festive snacks was a diamond-shaped sweet called **shankarpale** (a crisp fried Diwali snack). The host, however, offered it as "Please have some pale."

The reason? "Our Guru has forbidden us to say 'Shankar'. We are devotees of Lord Vishnu."

Acharya Ji explains that Lord Vishnu worships Shankar, and Shankar worships Lord Vishnu. Ganapati is a form of Lord Vishnu, and if you read His story you will find that He is the son of that very Shankar.

## Vaishnava, Shakta or Shaiva?

A seeker once brought a friend along, saying, "Here they teach about Ganesha, and they begin with the Bhagavad Gita." The friend decided this must be a **Vaishnava** (a follower of Lord Vishnu) teacher.

Then Navaratri came, the nine nights of the Devi, and the teaching turned to the Goddess, so the friend concluded he was a **Shakta** (a worshipper of the Divine Mother). Later the friend was invited for Shivaratri, the great night of Lord Shiva, and now he seemed a **Shaiva** (a devotee of Lord Shiva). In the end the friend reached a verdict: this could not possibly be a true Guru.

The confusion, however, lay not in the Guru but in the eyes that were looking.

## The True Yoga of the Ganesha Gita

In the very first chapter of the Ganesha Gita, Lord Ganesha Himself declares that He is everything:

> **aham eva mahāviṣṇur aham eva sadāśivaḥ |**
> **aham eva mahāśaktir aham evāryamā priya ||**
> "O dear one, I am Mahavishnu, I am Sadashiva, I am Mahashakti, and I am the Sun."
> (Ganesha Gita 1.19)

> **śive viṣṇau ca śaktau ca sūrye mayi narādhipa |**
> **yā'bhedabuddhir yogaḥ sa samyagyogo mato mama ||**
> "O King, the vision that sees no difference between Shiva, Vishnu, Shakti, the Sun and Me is what I hold to be true yoga."
> (Ganesha Gita 1.21)

Here the Lord is telling us that He is Shiva, He is the Devi, He is everything. Sanskrit calls this **abheda buddhi** (the understanding that sees no division).

This vision ends the quarrel over "your God" and "my God".

## Key Takeaways

- God takes form for the good of seekers, to help them grasp the formless; the form is not a place to stop.
- To "love" a form without knowing it is like marrying a stranger's photograph.
- Love is the foundation of devotion; a bond built on asking is self-interest.
- The Bhagavad Gita and the Ganesha Gita both teach: know God, don't just believe.
- The real search begins when you ask "why".
- Seeing no difference between Shiva, Vishnu, Shakti, the Sun and Ganesha is the true yoga of the Ganesha Gita.
