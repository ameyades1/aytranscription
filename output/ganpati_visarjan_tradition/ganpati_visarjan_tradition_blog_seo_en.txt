# What the Handful of Clay Returned After Visarjan Tells Us

## Ten Days, and Then What?

Lord Ganesha arrives, music plays for ten days, the decorations go up, and on the tenth day we declare that we no longer need the idol.

But what did we talk about during those ten days? Your Ganpati is bigger, ours is smaller, this decoration is lovely. If that is all the festival comes down to, what have we gained from such a beautiful symbol? Acharya Ji asks us to begin by understanding what the festival was meant to be and what we are actually doing.

So what is the meaning of Ganpati Visarjan? According to Acharya Upendra Ji, it is offering the idol's name and form to flowing water, the Ganga of knowledge, just as different ornaments melt into the same gold. The handful of clay that comes back is the same for everyone: it stands for the one God in all and teaches Advaita.

## Visarjan: Offering Name and Form to the Ganga of Knowledge

On the tenth day we did exactly what the rishis prescribed: **visarjan** (the ritual immersion of the idol). But what did they intend by it? They wanted each of us to dissolve our own **nama-rupa** (name and form, the identity that has a shape).

A simple example makes this clear. Your nose ring, your ring and your chain all look different, yet once melted down they become the same gold. In the same way, we offer the idol's name and form to flowing water, to a river, to the sea.

Flowing water stands for the Ganga, and here the Ganga means the **Gyan Ganga** (the Ganga of knowledge). Visarjan was meant to be a dip in that river of knowledge.

Along the riverbanks and the seashore you see thousands of idols: thousands of names, forms and bodies, each household with its own symbol. All of them merge into the same sea. In just the same way, one day all of us will merge into that formless state of knowledge, happiness, dispassion and truth, the complete truth that is **gunatita** (beyond the three gunas, the qualities that make up all of nature).

## The Same Clay in Every Hand

After the immersion, the clay drawn from the water is placed on your seat, on a low wooden stool, and handed to you to take home.

Now think about it for a moment. One idol wore black and another red. One was fair and another dark, one tall and another small. One was in the form of a Guru and another was plain; one took a woman's form and another wore half-pants. Each looked different when it went into the water. And when it came back? Every hand held exactly the same thing: a small heap of clay.

This carries a deep meaning. The clay is the substance everything is made of, and that substance is the same for all of us: **God**. On the tenth day, it was God we were meant to bring home. From that day on, we should have seen **Advaita** (non-duality, the experience of one reality in everything) all around us. Acharya Ji asks whether anyone receives twenty-two kilos of clay. Even the person with the biggest idol gets just a handful.

Whether your Ganpati stood twenty-two feet tall or fit in your palm, the clay that returns is a handful. Nobody weighs it on a scale and says, "Your idol was small, so you get a spoonful." Everyone receives exactly the same. The festival was meant to dissolve precisely this sense of big and small.

*Is a feeling of "mine is bigger, yours is smaller" hiding, somewhere in your own life, the one truth that is the same in everyone?*

## When the Festival Turns into a Competition

First prize, second prize, whose music was better, whose decorations were grander. Acharya Ji says plainly that all of this was pointless. **We were meant to embrace Advaita and move forward.**

That purpose got left far behind, and we never learned what really mattered.

## What to Do with the Clay After Visarjan?

Tradition asked us to spread this clay through the whole house. Homes used to be built of mud, so families mixed it into the plaster on the walls, and the truth it carried stayed with the household every day.

And today? The clay comes home and someone asks, "Mother, what do I do with this rubbish?" We carried it in chanting "Ganpati, Ganpati", and two moments later we wash it down the bathroom drain. As Acharya Ji puts it, two minutes earlier it was Ganpati, and now we throw it away as rubbish.

*How did something worthy of worship a few moments ago suddenly turn into rubbish? What changed: the clay, or the way we look at it?*

## Our Duty to Step Out of a Mistake

The scriptures say you are free to make mistakes, but deep down your heart knows a mistake for what it is. Your duty is to step out of it before it harms you or anyone else.

That is why Antar Yog teaches something different: teach your children, fully and from an early age, what Ganpati really means.

## Key Takeaways

- Visarjan symbolises offering name and form to the Ganga of knowledge.
- Flowing water stands for the Ganga, and the Ganga stands for knowledge.
- Everyone receives the same handful of clay, a symbol of the one God in all.
- From the tenth day on, we should see Advaita everywhere.
- Big or small idols, prizes and musical rivalry are not the purpose of the festival.
- Give the clay a place in your home; never wash it down the drain as rubbish.
- Teach children from an early age what Ganpati means.
