# Chapter: Act from Happiness — One Key to a Successful Life


## Introduction: One Task, Two Outlooks

All our lives we keep doing something — a job, a business, running a household, even marriage. But according to Acharya Upendra ji, the real question is not *what* we do, but *in what spirit* we do it. He points to a simple distinction that can change the direction of an entire life:

> "There are two ways of doing work — doing it in order to become happy, and doing it out of happiness."


## 1. The First Way: Working in Order to Become Happy

Most people live the first way. Acharya ji explains it in our everyday language:

> "What does doing it to become happy mean? I want a salary, I want a promotion, so I am doing it. In return for this I am going to get that, so I am doing this."

This spirit of a deal does not stay limited to the job — it seeps into relationships too:

> "I am even getting married so that I get so and so some joy — that is why I am doing it. So that my household runs, her salary will also get added. I am a doctor, she is a doctor — we get married and our hospital will run."

**Where the calculation of "what will I get in return" begins, work turns into a transaction.**


## 2. Expectation and Self-Interest: The Seed of Sorrow

Acharya ji clearly identifies the problem hidden in this first way:

> "Now expectation has come into it. So what will happen in this? There is no unconditional love. What did I see in it? My own benefit — self-interest has come in."

And he states the result without any softening:

> "Now I am doing this in order to become happy, so I cannot be happy in it. After certain time, only sorrow is going to come."

A happiness that rests on some outer reward comes and goes with that reward. **Work done from expectation ultimately brings only sorrow.**


## 3. The Second Way: Working Out of Happiness

Now Acharya ji shows the second path — where joy is not the *result* of the work, but its *starting point*:

> "What is the second way? It is to do it out of happiness. Meaning, if I have to do business, it is not to get money. I know how to make one thing, or I know how to offer one service — that itself is my passion."

> "I should feel joy in it anyway, without money also. Now I am simply sharing it with others."

He makes it clearer with the example of a painter:

> "I am a painter; I am good at painting anyway. So if I give my paintings to others as well, I will also get money for it. So I am not doing it in order to become happy — I am doing it out of happiness."

> "The joy was already there, without anyone giving reward or anything. So that is the profession you should take up."

**The work that gives you joy even without any reward — that is your true calling.** Money still comes, but as a natural result, not as the goal.


## 4. The Vision of the Bhagavad Gita: From Passion to Karma Yoga

Acharya ji then lifts this principle to a spiritual height:

> "This is mentioned in the Bhagavad Gita — that one who does only the work of their passion — do what you like and like what you do — can move towards moksha; he qualifies for Karma Yoga."

*Karma Yoga* means making action itself a yoga — acting without attachment to its fruits, so that the same action becomes a means of liberation (*moksha*) instead of bondage. According to Acharya ji, its sign is simple:

> "Do work that feels like play. Work should not feel like work, it should feel like play — like that. It should feel like happiness; there should be joy in it."

**When work starts to feel like play, it becomes Karma Yoga — and Karma Yoga leads towards liberation.**


## 5. At the Root Is Unconditional Love

Where does this joy come from? Acharya ji names its source — *unconditional love*:

> "So it cannot happen at all without unconditional love — unconditional love for the one you are giving to, and for yourself as well."

> "While you are doing it, you yourself should also enjoy it."

This love flows in both directions — towards those we are doing it for, and towards ourselves. **True love for others cannot flow into a work that we ourselves do not enjoy.**


## 6. An Example from the Home: The Woman Who Cooks

Acharya ji explains this principle through a simple example from household life:

> "So the woman who enjoys cooking — she enjoys it anyway — will cook well at her in-laws' home, and will cook well here too."

> "Initially she should be happy. It is to be done out of happiness, not in order to become happy — not with the thought 'if I do this I will get this, if I do that I will get that.' It is by default — that is simply the work they do."

One whose joy comes from within does not change their work when circumstances change — whether at her parents' home or her in-laws', she does it with the same love. **The joy within decides the quality of the work outside.**


## 7. Acharya Ji's Own Example

Finally, Acharya ji points to his own life — he lives what he teaches:

> "I love to teach, but I also love to learn. So I keep learning new things, and I keep giving you what I have learned. So it's better that I do that."

Learning and teaching — both are joy for him, not means to some reward. This is the living form of "acting out of happiness."


## Summary: Key Teachings

1. **There are two ways to work** — doing it in order to become happy, and doing it out of happiness.
2. **Work done in order to become happy** — for salary, promotion or gain — is full of expectation and self-interest, and after some time brings only sorrow.
3. **Work done out of happiness** is the work that gives joy even without money or reward — that is your passion and your true profession.
4. **Do what you like and like what you do** — when work starts to feel like play, it becomes Karma Yoga and leads towards liberation.
5. **Its root is unconditional love** — for those you are doing it for, and for yourself as well.
6. **Let the inner joy come first, and the action after** — then, whatever the circumstances, the work is done with the same love and quality.
